# Prompt Craft

How to word what goes on the page. These rules come from ideation and reflection research; the short justification is kept with each rule so it can be reasoned about rather than followed blindly. Full sourcing in `evidence.md`.

## 1. Fixation is the central design problem, and it runs both ways

Showing someone an example makes their ideas resemble it. This is the most replicated finding in design cognition. It reduces both fluency and the number of distinct categories explored, and it happens even when the example's flaws are pointed out.

Three consequences that are less obvious and more useful:

**Telling people not to copy does not work.** Groups instructed to avoid the example incorporated its features at the same rate as groups given no instruction at all. Encouraging instructions change behavior; discouraging instructions mostly don't. **So the sheet's layout must do the work, not its warnings.** Never write "try not to be influenced by the above." It buys nothing and it wastes attention.

**The blank page does not save you either.** People fixate on their own first idea, in at least one study *more* strongly than the example group fixated on the provided example. A page with no scaffolding just hands fixation to whatever the human thought of first.

**Abstraction is the escape hatch.** Naming the common solutions *specifically* and asking people to avoid them increases fixation. Naming the same common solutions as *categories* and asking people to go elsewhere mitigates fixation and roughly doubles creative output. This is the single most actionable finding in the literature for this skill.

### The rules that follow

- **Never print more than one concrete example in a generative zone.** If AI contributes an example, exactly one, and it should be remote or uncommon rather than typical. Meta-analytic evidence: fewer and less common examples increase novelty and quality; common examples produce the most copying.
- **Prefer categories to instances.** Instead of three sample answers, print the three or four obvious *territories* and mark them spent. Use the `Spent Ground` pattern.
- **Quarantine the human's own first answers.** Generative sheets get a small "the obvious ones, get them out" box before the real zone. Naming your defaults is what lets you leave them.
- **Never print AI-generated imagery on an ideation sheet.** Direct evidence: participants supported by an AI image generator during ideation showed higher fixation and produced fewer ideas with less variety and lower originality than baseline.

## 2. Separate generation from selection, always

People are bad at picking their own best ideas. In controlled comparison, idea selection was **no better than chance**, and there is a consistent bias toward the *feasible* over the *original*. Generating and selecting also want opposite mental postures.

- Generation and selection never share a zone, and preferably not a page.
- On any selection zone, include one counterweight against the feasibility bias. A single line does it: "One of your picks has to be the one you're not sure you can pull off." Make it a house rule, not a paragraph.
- If the sheet's job is selection, do not also ask for new ideas in the margin. That's what open territory is for.

## 3. The good ideas arrive late, so build for volume

Idea generation has a known temporal shape: people start in the most common categories and only move to novel categories once the common ones are exhausted. Stopping early guarantees ordinary output.

- **Give more slots than feel comfortable.** Ten short lines, not four. Empty slots at the end are not failure; they're the shape of the search.
- **Number the slots**, and let the later ones carry slightly more visual weight, because that is where novelty lives.
- **State both goals, neither binding.** A non-binding quantity goal raises quantity without hurting quality; a non-binding creativity goal raises creativity without hurting quantity. Both, stated plainly: "Aim for twelve. At least two should be ideas you'd be embarrassed to say out loud."

## 4. Constrain the input, never the clock

Constraints help creativity. A meta-analysis of 111 studies found a significant positive relationship, and field experiment evidence shows moderate constraints raise both quantity and quality, with an inverse-U beyond that. But the benefit of input constraints holds **only when people are not also under time pressure**, and screen-inferiority research separately shows time pressure is what triggers shallow processing.

- **Every generative zone carries at least one input constraint.** A required word, a forbidden category, a fixed format, a materials limit, a point of view.
- **Never print a time limit, a countdown, a suggested duration, or a "5 min" label.** This is a hard rule and it contradicts most worksheet convention. Ambiguous time increases creativity without reducing output.
- **Introduce constraints before the prompt, not after.** Constraints introduced before problem identification improve final solutions; tacked-on constraints just annoy.
- Moderate, not maximal. One or two real constraints per zone. Piling them on crosses the inverse-U.

## 5. Introspection prompts: distance and both channels

For reflective and introspective sheets specifically:

- **Ask for thoughts and feelings together.** Writing about emotions *and* thoughts produced better outcomes than either alone: higher wellbeing, more resolution, less residual anger.
- **Write prompts in the distanced voice.** The mechanism behind expressive writing's benefits is self-distancing, and linguistically it shows up as more causal language and fewer first-person-singular pronouns. So prefer "What would you tell someone standing where you're standing?" over "How do you feel about this?" Prefer "What made this happen?" over "Why are you upset?"
- **Keep introspective sessions short.** The paradigm that works is roughly 15 to 20 minutes of writing, repeated, not one marathon.
- Never combine an introspection zone with a ranking or productivity zone on the same sheet. Different postures, and the productivity frame will win.

## 6. Wording mechanics

- **One question per zone.** Compound questions get one answer, usually to the easier half.
- **Open the question, close the format.** "What is this class for?" with ten short lines beats "List three purposes of this class."
- **No hedging in prompts.** "Maybe consider thinking about what might matter" is noise. "What matters here?" is a question.
- **Second person, present tense.** "You're eight weeks in and it isn't working. What did you skip?"
- **Ban encouragement.** No "Great work so far!", no exclamation marks, no "Have fun with this one." It reads as a system talking down to a person and it costs credibility on a page that is otherwise honest.
- **Name the stakes when they exist.** "This decision sets the next six months" is useful pressure. "This is important!" is not.
- **The intent line is the loop's only stateless carrier.** The one-sentence intent in the page-1 header must be written so a cold reader could resume the session from it alone — name the task and what the sheet decides, not the mood. "Re-sequence the eight studio sessions around a taste-building arc," never "Thinking about the course." Scans come back days later, sometimes to a chat that no longer holds the session; when that happens, the intent line is most of what survives, and `scan-back` reconstructs from it.

## 7. Selection: fast, named, and distant

Selection has real fixes, and they run against instinct. Deliberative selection performs at chance; **intuitive selection beats it** on originality and matches it on usefulness. Rubrics do not improve accuracy. Rank-ordering mechanics (choosing versus elimination versus paired comparison) make no difference at all, with Bayesian support for the null.

- **No scoring matrices, no weighted criteria, no rubrics.** They add effort and buy nothing. A selection zone is a fast gut pick with room for a one-line reason.
- **Name the criterion in the prompt.** "Pick the two most original" outperforms "pick the best." Explicitly instructing for originality is the *only* instruction manipulation shown to work. Expect the person to feel less satisfied with what they pick; that is the effect working, not failing.
- **Add psychological distance.** Greater distance raises the originality of what gets selected, mediated by construal level. "Choose as the person who has to teach this a year from now" is a free upgrade over "choose."
- **Never mention implementation on a selection sheet.** In a 403-person field experiment, telling people they would have to build what they picked shifted them away from original ideas toward feasible ones. No "which one will you ship," no "pick what you can execute by Friday." Execution planning goes on a different sheet.
- **Don't engineer the ranking mechanism.** Spend the design effort on the criterion wording instead.

## 8. Analogy: the machine's best generative contribution

Cross-domain analogy is the one place where AI-generated content on a sheet has direct empirical support. LLM-produced cross-domain analogies were rated helpful at a median of 4 out of 5 and shifted how people framed the problem in roughly 80% of cases.

The distance tradeoff is clean: **far analogies raise novelty, near analogies raise quality.** Match distance to what the sheet is for.

- **One analogy per zone, stated in one line.** Comprehensive explanation erases the advantage; shallow framing preserves it.
- **Far and uncommon together** is the strongest combination for novelty. Note this is the same conclusion the fixation literature reaches about examples, arriving independently.
- **Too far is harmful.** If the source domain is genuinely alien, add a **bridge**: name something familiar that sits between the person's knowledge and the far source. Bridged far analogies produce more abstract solutions than raw far analogies.
- **Biological sources punch above their weight** for novelty, especially when explained briefly.
- **Near analogies for people new to the domain.** Far-analogy benefit assumes domain knowledge; without it, near sources produce both higher novelty and higher quality.

## 9. Effort will be misread, and the sheet should account for it

Across three studies, people rated more effortful strategies as *less* effective and were less likely to choose them, while the effortful strategy actually produced better retention. A paper session will feel less productive than the same hour on screen, including on the days it is most productive.

Two consequences:

- **Never manufacture difficulty in the typography.** Hard-to-read fonts are a debunked application of an otherwise sound idea, and where any effect exists it follows an inverted-U and reverses on complex material. Difficulty belongs in the task. The page stays maximally legible.
- **Do not promise ease, and do not apologize for effort.** "This will take a while and it should" is honest and sets the expectation correctly. Cheerful minimizing ("quick exercise!") sets up the exact misread that makes people abandon the method.
