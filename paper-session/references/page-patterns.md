# Page Pattern Library

Choose 1-3 patterns per session. Each entry notes capacity (Deep/Light), whose thought fills it, and the evidence behind its shape. Read `prompt-craft.md` before wording any prompt on these.

Universal requirements, from the research:
- Every generative zone carries **one input constraint** and **no time limit**.
- Generation and selection never share a zone.
- Slot counts run higher than feels comfortable; novelty arrives after the obvious categories are exhausted.
- At least one zone per Deep kit invites **drawing**, not just prose. Drawn content consistently beats handwritten content on recall, across multiple studies including ones that found no handwriting advantage at all.

## Defixation patterns (use one on any generative sheet)

**Spent Ground.** The highest-value pattern in the library. AI lists the three to five obvious solution **categories** at an abstract level, marked as already-walked, followed by the open generative zone. Not specific examples: categories. Naming common solutions specifically and asking people to avoid them *increases* fixation; naming them as categories roughly doubles creative output. AI fills the category list; everything below is human-only.

**First Three Are Free.** A small boxed zone at the top of a generative sheet: "The obvious answers. Write them here so they stop taking up room." Then the real zone below. People fixate on their own first idea at least as hard as on a provided example, so the blank page is not a neutral starting condition. Human-only, both halves.

**The Single Remote Example.** When AI genuinely should seed the space, exactly **one** example, chosen to be uncommon or from a distant domain, set in the machine's voice. Meta-analytic evidence: fewer and less common examples increase both novelty and quality, while common examples produce the most copying. Never two. Never typical.

## Sequencing patterns (Deep)

**Cut-apart cards.** One item per card, title plus one-line promise, 6-8 cards per page with cut lines, plus two blank cards. The human cuts them apart and arranges them on a table until the arc appears. AI fills cards that summarize existing material; arrangement is human-only. The blank cards matter: without them the set reads as closed.

*Scope this honestly.* Physical manipulation does **not** reliably improve problem solving in general; controlled studies find no facilitating effect on insight problems, and one found physical interaction actively hindered use of hints. The benefit appears when manipulation reveals structure that is hard to hold in the head: many items, relational or spatial arrangement, load beyond working memory. Course arcs, campaign phasing, and narrative order qualify. A four-item list does not. Use cards when the arrangement is the problem, not as a general creativity booster.

**Numbered slots, empty.** A vertical list of numbered blank slots filled in order. Use when the items themselves are the human's to invent, not just their order.

## Weighing and selection patterns (Deep)

**Two-column react.** AI's proposal in the machine's voice on the left, empty right column for re-ranking, corrections, and margin fights. The human writing "no" next to a confident machine answer is the loop working.

**The kill sheet.** Everything in scope on the left; a right column with room for exactly half. The physical constraint does the cutting. AI fills the left; the right is human-only.

**Impact grid.** A large 2x2 with axes chosen for the task. Items pre-listed as small labels to place, or blank to populate. Placement is human-only.

**Selection counterweight (required on all selection zones).** One line, house-rule voice, near the picks: "One of these has to be the one you're not sure you can pull off." Idea selection performs no better than chance in controlled study, with a consistent bias toward feasible over original. The counterweight is cheap insurance against that bias.

## Generative patterns (Deep)

**The long list.** A single prompt over **ten to fourteen** numbered short lines, with one stated input constraint and a two-part non-binding goal ("Aim for twelve. At least two should be ones you'd be embarrassed to say out loud."). Slot count is the whole design: the common categories get exhausted in the first third, and novelty lives in the back half. Human-only.

**One provocation per page.** A single hard question at the top, the rest of the page empty. For questions that deserve a page of wandering. Use three to five of these as an entire kit when the work is genuinely conceptual.

**Constraint box.** A box that physically fits about twelve words, with the constraint stated above it. The limit does the editing.

**Sketch field (required somewhere in every Deep kit).** A dot-grid zone with a prompt that asks for a drawing, diagram, or spatial arrangement rather than sentences: "Draw the shape of the arc." "Sketch what the room looks like in week eight." Drawing outperforms writing on recall, and it recruits a different retrieval path than prose.

## Light patterns

**Rank-and-circle.** AI's list, each with a rank box and room for a one-word note. Circle keeps, cross out kills. Carries the selection counterweight.

**Gut-check ladder.** Items down the left, a simple scale per item. One pen-stroke each.

**Reaction margins.** A short AI-drafted artifact printed with wide margins and double spacing, for slash-and-scribble.

## Introspection patterns (Deep, own sheet)

**Distanced pair.** Two zones on one sheet: what happened and what you make of it, then what it felt like. Writing about thoughts *and* feelings beats either alone. Prompts written in the distanced voice ("What would you tell someone standing where you're standing?"), since self-distancing is the mechanism that makes reflective writing work. Never share a sheet with ranking or planning zones.

## Field patterns (away from desk)

**Observation log.** Timestamp, observation, and a "why it matters" column.

**Tally sheet.** Predefined categories with tally space plus an uncategorized overflow zone.

## Universal closer (every sheet)

**Open territory.** An unstructured zone, minimum a third of the final page. Never omit. The margins and the wandering are frequently where the best material comes back from.

## Selection patterns (new; use one whenever a kit generates options)

**Fast pick.** The options listed plainly, a named criterion in the prompt, and room for one line of reason per pick. No scores, no matrices, no rubrics. Deliberative selection performs at chance while intuitive selection beats it, so the zone should invite a gut call and then get out of the way. The criterion must be named ("the two most original," not "the best"), because that is the only selection instruction shown to work. Add a distance frame: "Choose as the person who has to live with this a year from now." Never mention building, shipping, or executing on this sheet; the prospect of implementation measurably shifts people toward safe picks.

## Analogy patterns

**One far door.** A single cross-domain analogy in the machine's voice, one line, no elaboration, followed by an open generative zone: "Where else does this problem already have a solved version?" Far analogies raise novelty, near ones raise quality, so pick the distance to match the sheet's job. If the source domain is genuinely alien to the person, add a one-line **bridge** naming something familiar in between. Deep explanation erases the effect; keep it to a sentence.

## Sketch patterns (expanded; this is the strongest paper-specific mechanism available)

**Sketch field.** A dot-grid zone with a prompt asking for a drawing, diagram, or spatial arrangement rather than sentences. Beyond the recall advantage, loose marks fight fixation directly: vagueness invites reinterpretation. Keep the prompt loose enough that the drawing can come out ambiguous, because the ambiguity is the working part.

**Second reading (pair with any sketch field).** Below the drawing zone, one line: "Now look at what you drew and find a reading of it you did not intend." This is trainable, not innate. Novices instructed in this skill generated more interpretations of an ambiguous drawing and kept generating longer than uninstructed novices. It is the cheapest upgrade in this library.

**Regroup.** "Circle two things on the page that you have never thought about at the same time. What do they make together?" Unexpected discovery is most likely when attention lands on elements never previously attended to together. Works on a fresh sketch or on a page already covered in the person's own marks.

**Structure sketch.** For any kit with multiple parts: "Draw these as a structure. Mark what carries the weight. Cross out what turns out to be decoration." Doubles as a selection zone, and answers relational questions that prose cannot reach.
