# Page Pattern Library

Choose 1-3 patterns per session. Each entry notes capacity (Deep/Light), whose thought fills it, and the evidence behind its shape. Read `prompt-craft.md` before wording any prompt on these.

Universal requirements, from the research:
- Every generative zone carries **one input constraint** and **no time limit**.
- Generation and selection never share a zone.
- Slot counts run higher than feels comfortable; novelty arrives after the obvious categories are exhausted.
- At least one zone per Deep kit invites **drawing**, not just prose. Drawn content consistently beats handwritten content on recall, across multiple studies including ones that found no handwriting advantage at all.

## Defixation patterns (use one on any generative sheet)

**Spent Ground.** The highest-value pattern in the library. AI lists the three to five obvious solution **categories** at an abstract level, marked as already-walked, followed by the open generative zone. Not specific examples: categories. Naming common solutions specifically and asking people to avoid them *increases* fixation; naming them as categories roughly doubles creative output. AI fills the category list; everything below is human-only.

**First Three Are Free.** A small boxed zone at the top of a generative sheet: "The obvious answers. Write them here so they stop taking up room." Then the real zone below. People fixate on their own first idea at least as hard as on a provided example, so the blank page is not a neutral starting condition. Human-only, both halves.

**The Single Remote Example.** When AI genuinely should seed the space, exactly **one** example, chosen to be uncommon or from a distant domain, set in the machine's voice. Meta-analytic evidence: fewer and less common examples increase both novelty and quality, while common examples produce the most copying. Never two. Never typical.

## Sequencing patterns (Deep)

**Cut-apart cards.** One item per card, title plus one-line promise, 6-8 cards per page with cut lines, plus two blank cards. The human cuts them apart and arranges them on a table until the arc appears. AI fills cards that summarize existing material; arrangement is human-only. The blank cards matter: without them the set reads as closed.

*Scope this honestly.* Physical manipulation does **not** reliably improve problem solving in general; controlled studies find no facilitating effect on insight problems, and one found physical interaction actively hindered use of hints. The benefit appears when manipulation reveals structure that is hard to hold in the head: many items, relational or spatial arrangement, load beyond working memory. Course arcs, campaign phasing, and narrative order qualify. A four-item list does not. Use cards when the arrangement is the problem, not as a general creativity booster.

**Numbered slots, empty.** A vertical list of numbered blank slots filled in order. Use when the items themselves are the human's to invent, not just their order.

## Weighing and selection patterns (Deep)

**Two-column react.** AI's proposal in the machine's voice on the left, empty right column for re-ranking, corrections, and margin fights. The human writing "no" next to a confident machine answer is the loop working.

**The kill sheet.** Everything in scope on the left; a right column with room for exactly half. The physical constraint does the cutting. AI fills the left; the right is human-only.

**Impact grid.** A large 2x2 with axes chosen for the task. Items pre-listed as small labels to place, or blank to populate. Placement is human-only.

**Selection counterweight (required on all selection zones).** One line, house-rule voice, near the picks: "One of these has to be the one you're not sure you can pull off." Idea selection performs no better than chance in controlled study, with a consistent bias toward feasible over original. The counterweight is cheap insurance against that bias.

## Generative patterns (Deep)

**The long list.** A single prompt over **ten to fourteen** numbered short lines, with one stated input constraint and a two-part non-binding goal ("Aim for twelve. At least two should be ones you'd be embarrassed to say out loud."). Slot count is the whole design: the common categories get exhausted in the first third, and novelty lives in the back half. Human-only.

**One provocation per page.** A single hard question at the top, the rest of the page empty. For questions that deserve a page of wandering. Use three to five of these as an entire kit when the work is genuinely conceptual.

**Constraint box.** A box that physically fits about twelve words, with the constraint stated above it. The limit does the editing.

**Sketch field (required somewhere in every Deep kit).** A dot-grid zone with a prompt that asks for a drawing, diagram, or spatial arrangement rather than sentences: "Draw the shape of the arc." "Sketch what the room looks like in week eight." Drawing outperforms writing on recall, and it recruits a different retrieval path than prose.

## Light patterns

**Rank-and-circle.** AI's list, each with a rank box and room for a one-word note. Circle keeps, cross out kills. Carries the selection counterweight.

**Gut-check ladder.** Items down the left, a simple scale per item. One pen-stroke each.

**Reaction margins.** A short AI-drafted artifact printed with wide margins and double spacing, for slash-and-scribble.

## Introspection patterns (Deep, own sheet)

**Distanced pair.** Two zones on one sheet: what happened and what you make of it, then what it felt like. Writing about thoughts *and* feelings beats either alone. Prompts written in the distanced voice ("What would you tell someone standing where you're standing?"), since self-distancing is the mechanism that makes reflective writing work. Never share a sheet with ranking or planning zones.

## Field patterns (away from desk)

**Observation log.** Timestamp, observation, and a "why it matters" column.

**Tally sheet.** Predefined categories with tally space plus an uncategorized overflow zone.

## Universal closer (every sheet)

**Open territory.** An unstructured zone, minimum a third of the final page. Never omit. The margins and the wandering are frequently where the best material comes back from.

## Selection patterns (new; use one whenever a kit generates options)

**Fast pick.** The options listed plainly, a named criterion in the prompt, and room for one line of reason per pick. No scores, no matrices, no rubrics. Deliberative selection performs at chance while intuitive selection beats it, so the zone should invite a gut call and then get out of the way. The criterion must be named ("the two most original," not "the best"), because that is the only selection instruction shown to work. Add a distance frame: "Choose as the person who has to live with this a year from now." Never mention building, shipping, or executing on this sheet; the prospect of implementation measurably shifts people toward safe picks.

## Analogy patterns

**One far door.** A single cross-domain analogy in the machine's voice, one line, no elaboration, followed by an open generative zone: "Where else does this problem already have a solved version?" Far analogies raise novelty, near ones raise quality, so pick the distance to match the sheet's job. If the source domain is genuinely alien to the person, add a one-line **bridge** naming something familiar in between. Deep explanation erases the effect; keep it to a sentence.

## Sketch patterns (expanded; this is the strongest paper-specific mechanism available)

**Sketch field.** A dot-grid zone with a prompt asking for a drawing, diagram, or spatial arrangement rather than sentences. Beyond the recall advantage, loose marks fight fixation directly: vagueness invites reinterpretation. Keep the prompt loose enough that the drawing can come out ambiguous, because the ambiguity is the working part.

**Second reading (pair with any sketch field).** Below the drawing zone, one line: "Now look at what you drew and find a reading of it you did not intend." This is trainable, not innate. Novices instructed in this skill generated more interpretations of an ambiguous drawing and kept generating longer than uninstructed novices. It is the cheapest upgrade in this library.

**Regroup.** "Circle two things on the page that you have never thought about at the same time. What do they make together?" Unexpected discovery is most likely when attention lands on elements never previously attended to together. Works on a fresh sketch or on a page already covered in the person's own marks.

**Structure sketch.** For any kit with multiple parts: "Draw these as a structure. Mark what carries the weight. Cross out what turns out to be decoration." Doubles as a selection zone, and answers relational questions that prose cannot reach.

## Named session formats (whole-session kits; choose by name)

Whole-session structures with real lineages — the after-action review out of Army debriefing practice, the premortem out of decision research, the field kit out of museum natural history. Choose one by name when the task matches its trigger, rather than assembling zones from scratch. Each composes patterns this library already has and adds only what its own evidence licenses; machine-filled zones carry the I PROPOSE caption as everywhere else. Every format has a named cluster in `evidence.md` (evidence brief, after-action review cluster, and so on), and any exception a format states to a library default — the premortem's slot cap against the overbuild rule, the outside view's pre-ticked anchor — is sourced there; an exception without its source is drift, not design. Entries run longer than zone patterns because each specifies a whole kit.

**After-action review.** Deep, 2-3 pages, after a project phase or a completed agentic session that left a recorded plan and execution log. The printed record is a hard condition, not decoration: written reflection without an objective record to react to produces no improvement at all, and the record is what qualifies this sheet for its evidence base. Page 1: the plan as recorded ("what was supposed to happen") and, where the machine executed steps, a bare factual timeline with no interpretation, in the machine's voice, with a two-column react beside it under the line "The log is not the whole truth." Page 2: the gap zone — "Why the difference?" — human-only; the machine withholds all diagnosis. Then twin sustain/change lists covering successes as well as failures; after a success, reviewing what went wrong anyway is the working part. The four canonical questions are the fixed structure — what was supposed to happen, what happened, why the difference, what to sustain and change — and high structure outperforms low. Nothing gets ranked here: prioritization, if wanted, is a later Fast pick, and next actions get drafted on screen after scan-back. Sketch field: "Draw the timeline where it bent," paired with Second reading. Never offer it as emotional processing after a distressing event — that literature runs null-to-harm, and introspection belongs to the Distanced pair. No record, no AAR; a trivial session with no gap gets no sheet. Sources: evidence brief, after-action review cluster.

**Premortem.** Deep, 2 pages, only when a concrete committed plan exists. Page 1: the live plan restated in one tight paragraph, machine's voice — genuine work worth reacting to. Then the certainty frame, asking voice: "It is a year from now. The plan failed — completely, not marginally. Write the history." Certainty is the active ingredient, not pastness; the wording must make the failure total and certain. Below it, **six to eight** numbered lines — a deliberate, sourced exception to the ten-to-fourteen overbuild rule. Overbuilding serves novelty; this sheet serves confidence calibration, and forcing many failure thoughts feels so difficult that the felt difficulty restores the very confidence the exercise exists to reduce. One input constraint: "At least three must be failures that are our own doing, not the world's" — the documented skew runs toward blaming the world. Page 2: sketch field, "Draw how the failure unfolded," with Second reading; then a separate Fast pick — circle the two most likely to be the real cause — with the counterweight adapted to "One circle must be the reason nobody would say in the meeting," because this sheet's bias is self-censorship, not feasibility. Countermeasures are deliberately absent from the sheet; the AI drafts them after scan-back, which closes the documented gap between naming failure reasons and revising the plan. No timer, no mitigation wording anywhere. Not for Light, not for an already-anxious planner — every supporting study targets overconfidence, and deflating the deflated has no support — and not for repeat runs on an unchanged plan. Sources: evidence brief, premortem cluster.

**Outside view.** Deep, 2 pages, for a timeline, budget, or adoption number the human is about to commit to. Page 1: the reference class in the machine's voice — comparable cases and base rates, with provenance stated on the page in one line; prefer the human's own past cases when they exist. Below it, a distribution strip on dot-grid with the base-rate anchor pre-ticked; the human marks where this case honestly sits. One pen position, and the spatial judgment is the point: prose lets you dodge it. The strip is the kit's sketch zone. Then the forced linkage, non-optional because it is the working part: "Write the short history of this project going the way the reference class typically goes." Displaying past cases beside an estimate does nothing; the linkage writing is the intervention. Page 2: the differences list — "What would have to be true for you to beat the base rate. Each line must be evidence you hold today, not an intention" — then the committed number, written once, that the human will defend. Promise calibration, never accuracy: the tested effect removes systematic optimism without sharpening any individual estimate. The pre-ticked anchor is licensed for this estimation format only and never leaks onto a generative sheet, where the same move is design fixation. Decline the format when no defensible reference class exists or the base rates' provenance cannot be stated: a fabricated distribution printed as an anchor is worse than no sheet. Sources: evidence brief, outside view cluster.

**Teach-back sheet.** Deep, 2 pages, closed-book by design. Offer it only after the human has genuinely engaged a body of conceptual explanation — an hour of AI-explained material is the canonical trigger — and never after a skim: closed-book prompts after passive processing measurably hurt, because nothing is loaded to retrieve. Page 1: one Serif line — "Explain X, from memory, to someone who has never met it" — over a full ruled page, with a narrow gap column: "Mark the lines where you reached for a word you couldn't cash out." Ask for mechanism, how and why it works, never a list of reasons; the deflation of inflated understanding requires mechanistic explanation and does not happen otherwise. Page 2: dot-grid — "Draw the mechanism from memory" — with Second reading; at most three retrieval probes in the machine's voice, no answers. The machine withholds every scrap of its own explanation, including material it obviously has: printing the content converts a calibration instrument into a recognition test, and paper is the enforcement the screen cannot provide. Closed-book is the calibration instrument, not the only route to retention. Gap marks are annotation, never scoring. The return trip is the feedback event, under the one scoped exception to scan-back's authority rule: the handwritten explanation is authoritative as a record of what the human believes, not as fact, so scan-back checks it against the source, corrects factual errors on return, and re-explains the gap-marked pieces differently. One sheet is one retrieval event, not a retention program. Wrong tool for procedural or worked-example material, and never a substitute for a generative sheet. Sources: evidence brief, teach-back cluster.

**Weekly review.** Recurring ritual, Deep, 2 pages — the weekly self-meeting the skill description already solicits. Page 1: the inventory of open loops the machine can actually see — chat threads, task lists, calendar — laid out two-column react; the human triages with the pen protocol as designed: strike kills, green go, blue do, red review. These are reaction marks on machine work-product, rank-and-circle territory, not selection over creative options, so no selection counterweight prints; feasibility bias has no originality to corrupt here. Never quadrants, never a priority matrix, never scoring of any kind. Page 2: the mind-sweep, a human-only long list — "What has your attention that no system knows about?" — with the machine-visible loops marked as Spent Ground categories so the sweep goes where the machine cannot see; then a structure sketch: "Draw next week as a structure. Mark what carries the weight." No next-action or if-then wording prints anywhere on the sheet. Capturing undone tasks without then planning them leaves the intrusions intact, so the continuation after scan-back is load-bearing, not cleanup: next actions get drafted there, kept deliberately lightweight — elaborately batch-planning many goals in one sitting undermines the planning benefit — and the machine transcribes the human's plans, never audits them. The mind-sweep is never pre-filled: the machine's inventory is precisely not the human's mind, and printing guesses there is the design-fixation failure mode. Weekly, not daily. Never fused with introspection; the Distanced pair keeps its own sheet. Skip the format when the machine holds no genuine visible inventory — never invent loops. Sources: evidence brief, weekly review cluster.

**Grinnell field kit.** Deep **series** — the library's first multi-day format, and the multi-day sibling of the Observation log. One kit per sitting, up to 4 pages per kit: the Deep page cap counts per kit, not per project, and more entities means more sessions, not more pages. For projects with recurring entities worth tracking across days — stakeholders, subsystems, failure modes. Page 1, the journal: a printed header of labeled blanks the human fills at the time of writing — date, location, conditions — then a ruled narrative zone in the fact-forward register: what happened, who, where, with human-written times as data. Times are quantification, not schedule; the Observation log's timestamp column is the precedent. A dot-grid band runs beside the narrative for sketch maps and diagrams — the lineage's own instruction, and it satisfies the sketch requirement natively. Account pages, at most two per kit: one per recurring entity, the entity's name printed as the page header in the machine's voice — gathered inventory at category level, categories-not-instances territory — and every field below empty, human-only. A compact catalog closes the working pages: a numbered one-line register of the concrete artifacts captured this session — documents, links, quotes, samples. The journal is not a diary; the lineage's own doctrine excludes the reflective voice, so this kit never merges with the Distanced pair. The same-day writing discipline is real doctrine, and it lives in the chat framing and the human's practice; nothing on a sheet ever schedules, deadlines, or hurries. Kits return days apart and account pages accumulate: scan-back appends to an entity's running account across sessions rather than treating each scan as standalone, and reorders by the human-written dates, which are data. No generative zone, so no defixation pattern is required. Not for Light, not for ideation; a project with no recurring entities is already served by the Observation log. Sources: evidence brief, Grinnell field-note cluster.

## Notebook translation (when the session is dictated, not printed)

When the human cannot print, the session is dictated as a setup card to copy into whatever notebook they own (`prompt-craft.md`, "Dictating instead of printing"). Choose against this gate before designing anything: some patterns survive the copy intact, some survive only under a restriction, and some do not survive at all.

The restriction is one line: **machine handles never appear on a page carrying a generative zone.** Copying by hand is deeper exposure than glancing at a printed page — the working hypothesis, flagged as unsourced in `evidence.md` — which makes handles safe where reacting is the task and corrosive where generating is. It bans handles, not every machine word: a Spent Ground category is a fence, not material to work from.

**Translate near-verbatim.** Nothing load-bearing is lost in the copy.

- One provocation per page — the best case in the library. A question copied at the top of an empty page is the entire pattern.
- The long list — with one procedural rule standing in for the gravity of printed slots: number every line before writing in any of them. A page numbered as it fills stops where the obvious categories stop.
- Sketch field, and Second reading with it — arguably improved. Dot grid is what most notebooks already are, and the second-reading prompt is one line to copy.
- Constraint box — the human draws the box. A hand-drawn box fits what it fits, and the limit still does the editing.
- Distanced pair — the isolation a dedicated sheet gives it becomes a page turn. Start it on a fresh page, keep both zones there in order, and let no ranking or planning zone share it.
- First Three Are Free — both halves were always human-only, so there is nothing to dictate but the prompt.
- Spent Ground — through its own convention: the categories are written small and struck through as they are written, which delivers the already-walked fence in the pen protocol's own vocabulary. Categories-not-instances is unchanged. Dictate territories; an instance dictated here is the failure the pattern exists to prevent.

**Translate only through handles, and only onto a page carrying no generative zone.**

- Two-column react, Rank-and-circle, and the kill sheet each need machine work-product to push against. It crosses as numbered handles with the full items held in chat; the human rules one line down the page, or leaves the back half of each line empty. Light sessions get the same work offered in the chat instead, so in practice this is a Deep route.

**Do not translate.**

- Reaction margins. The pattern is space around an AI draft, and no draft reaches the page unless the human transcribes it whole — the one copy this gate exists to refuse.
- Cut-apart cards. A bound notebook cannot be cut. Index cards restore the scissors but not the pattern, since the cards would carry hand-copied machine summaries; cards work only where the items are the human's own to invent.

When the pattern you wanted lands here, choose a different pattern that does the job. Never invent a notebook substitute: it inherits the name of a mechanism and none of the evidence under it.
