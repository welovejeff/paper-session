# The Case for Offline Mode: Evidence Brief

Research support for the paper-session thesis, ordered by the priority ranking. Contested findings are flagged rather than buried, because a public thesis piece that overclaims gets dismantled in the comments, and the honest version of this argument is stronger than the hyped one.

---

## Cluster 1: Offline incubation and mind-wandering fuel creativity

**The anchor.** The definitive meta-analysis is Sio and Ormerod's [Does incubation enhance problem solving? A meta-analytic review](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c3ef39ddc0b95f2d969d5c76c56c089e/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Psychological Bulletin, 2009, 526 citations, DOI: 10.1037/a0014212). Four findings matter, and three of them are directly actionable for skill design:

1. There is a positive incubation effect, and **divergent thinking tasks benefit more than linguistic and visual insight tasks**. Idea generation gains most.
2. **Longer preparation periods produce a greater incubation effect.** Time spent loading the problem before you step away is not wasted; it is what makes stepping away pay.
3. **Filling the incubation period with high cognitive demand tasks produces a smaller effect.** This is the finding that should reshape how you talk about paper sessions (see "The tension" below).
4. Low cognitive demand tasks outperformed pure rest for linguistic insight problems. Doing something undemanding beats doing nothing.

**The headline experiment.** Baird et al., [Inspired by Distraction](https://consensus.app/papers/details/88546add3df25bfe9a486a5754388312/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Psychological Science, 2012, 633 citations, DOI: 10.1177/0956797612446024). Compared with a demanding task, rest, or no break at all, an **undemanding task that maximizes mind wandering produced substantial improvements** on previously encountered creativity problems. Critically, the benefit tracked mind wandering, not explicit directed thought about the problem.

**Supporting texture.** Ellwood et al., [The Incubation Effect: Hatching a Solution?](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9a328038b19954b2a8a7c1792054847d/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Creativity Research Journal, 2009) found a break spent on a *completely different* task beat both a similar task and continuous work. Segal's [Incubation in Insight Problem Solving](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c0cbf7835ec55e34a4ccf79b9c9edd39/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (2004) found breaks improve insight performance but **length doesn't matter**, and that the break works best when it starts *after* an impasse is reached. Irving et al.'s [shower effect](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6f70fa3dfb825f57b21ced01e039cef9/?utm_source=claude_desktop) paper (2022) supports moderately engaging activities as the sweet spot.

**The honest counterweight.** Murray et al., [What are the benefits of mind wandering to creativity?](https://consensus.app/papers/details/95c1c52443305bf6980464bd0f0d0a70/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (2021) attempted a conceptual replication of Baird across **two studies, N = 443, and found no evidence** that mind wandering during incubation facilitates divergent creativity. Du et al. (2025) similarly found mind wandering's effect "limited and context-dependent," with conscious reflection during incubation more beneficial than uncontrolled drifting.

**How to hold this honestly:** the *incubation* effect is meta-analytically solid. The *mind wandering as the mechanism* claim is genuinely contested. Argue the former, mention the latter as an open question. This actually helps you: the replication failures point toward deliberate reflection over passive drifting, which is exactly what a structured worksheet provides and a shower does not.

## Cluster 2: Paper beats screens for deliberation

**The meta-analytic core.** Clinton's [Reading from paper compared to screens](https://consensus.app/papers/details/52817a59c9f152cbb0d9195b5414cbaf/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Journal of Research in Reading, 2019, 342 citations, DOI: 10.1111/1467-9817.12269) synthesized 33 randomized studies (n = 2,799):

- Screen reading impaired performance relative to paper, **g = -0.25**
- The effect concentrated in **expository texts (g = -0.32)** with essentially nothing for narrative (g = -0.04)
- **No reliable difference in reading time** (g = 0.08), so this is not a speed tradeoff
- Readers' **judgment of their own performance was better calibrated on paper (g = 0.20)**

That last one is the most important number in this entire brief. On screen, people are not just worse; they are worse *and don't know it*.

Kong et al.'s [meta-analysis](https://consensus.app/papers/details/cc186adde72c58b3a6f8410aff37e2cf/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Computers & Education, 2018, 251 citations) independently confirms the comprehension advantage for paper with no speed difference. Salmerón et al. (2023) found the effect persists on handheld devices, though attenuated (g ≈ -0.11), which tells you a tablet recovers *some* of the gap.

**The mechanism is not the hardware.** This is the part worth teaching. Sidi et al., [Generalizing screen inferiority](https://consensus.app/papers/details/41a03b3a8305534a96e5fd89bebf889d/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Metacognition and Learning, 2016) used tasks with almost no reading burden at all and still found the effect, and their [follow-up](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b48b661e14a95d17989085f23a1fd66a/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Learning and Instruction, 2017) concluded that **the medium itself acts as a contextual cue that licenses shallower processing**, regardless of text length. Ackerman and Ackerman colleagues found screen inferiority appears specifically under time pressure and when perceived task importance drops. The screen doesn't degrade your eyes. It signals to your brain that this is a skimming context.

Hakemulder et al. (2024) extend this into the shallowing hypothesis: **more frequent short-form screen reading predicted less inclination to muster the cognitive persistence** required for longer texts and deeper contemplation.

**Boundary condition to disclose:** Hoch et al. (2025) found no screen inferiority in high-stakes university e-exams (n = 2,250), where deep processing was already guaranteed by the stakes. Screen inferiority is a default-mode effect, not a law of physics. If the context already forces depth, the medium matters less.

## Cluster 3: Handwriting beats typing for thinking

**Neural evidence.** Van der Weel and Van der Meer, [Handwriting but not typewriting leads to widespread brain connectivity](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5872a45833015cd0987faf8e4458c607/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Frontiers in Psychology, 2024, DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945). 256-channel EEG on 36 university students: **handwriting produced far more elaborate connectivity patterns** than keyboard typing, with widespread theta/alpha coherence between parietal and central hubs, the frequency bands associated with memory formation and encoding of new information. Ose Askvik et al. (2020) found the same pattern in both adults and 12-year-olds.

**Behavioral evidence.** Wiley and Rapp, [The Effects of Handwriting Experience on Literacy Learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0ebc94dc75045b56bba3ae1eb574bf4c/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Psychological Science, 2021): handwriting practice produced **faster learning and greater generalization to untrained tasks** than non-motor practice, and uniquely produced both motor *and* amodal symbolic representations. Ihara et al. (2021) found a larger N400 priming effect for handwritten words versus typed, plus significantly more positive mood during handwriting regardless of condition.

**The honest counterweight.** Richardson et al. (2023) replicated the drawing effect but **did not find handwriting superior to typing for free recall**, concluding that if the pen is mightier, the effect isn't explained by visual attention or sensorimotor differences. A commentary by Pinet et al. (2025) in Frontiers pushes back on the connectivity paper.

**How to hold this:** the encoding and generalization evidence is strong; the simple "you'll remember more" claim is shakier. Also note the drawing finding, which recurs everywhere: **drawn content beat handwritten content on recall.** That is an argument for making more of your sheets invite sketching, not just prose.

## Cluster 4: The cost of staying on screen

**Attention residue.** Leroy's [Why is it so hard to do my work?](https://consensus.app/papers/details/382d5ad290775351a82fab7d5ea28824/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2009, 289 citations) established that people must stop thinking about one task to perform well on another, and that **transitioning away from an unfinished task is difficult and measurably degrades subsequent performance**. Her 2018 Organization Science follow-up shows a brief "ready-to-resume" plan mitigates it.

**The state of the modern workday.** Talypova et al. (2025, ACM Symposium on HCI for Work) recorded 15 knowledge workers in naturalistic one-hour sessions: participants **deviated from the main task every 3.5 minutes on average**, with nearly **60% of off-task time self-initiated**, and average focused attention within the main working sphere down to **about 6 minutes**, a significant decrease from prior decades.

**The most useful single study for your argument.** Schweisfurth et al., [Unexpected Interruptions, Idle Time, and Creativity](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e748f51592ba54318c2d72e55dbf5a15/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Organization Science, 2023) exploited a supply chain shortage as a natural experiment: employees hit by an unexpected interruption **that created idle time produced 58% more ideas** in the following three weeks than uninterrupted employees. Crucially, the effect did **not** appear for intrusions without idle time, and did **not** appear for planned breaks (where people mentally clock out to leisure goals). The mechanism they land on is attention residue: the unfinished problem keeps working on you only if the interruption leaves your mind on the problem rather than reassigning it.

That is precisely the design of a paper session. Not a vacation, not an intrusion. An interruption that leaves the problem live.

## Cluster 5: What makes this urgent now (the agentic-era case)

This is the material that makes the piece a 2026 argument rather than a timeless one about notebooks.

**AI use correlates with reduced critical thinking.** Gerlich, [AI Tools in Society](https://consensus.app/papers/details/886650bd2bc550a49677a8ce40a93d10/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Societies, 2025, 853 citations, n = 666) found a **significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool use and critical thinking, mediated by cognitive offloading**, with younger participants showing higher dependence and lower scores.

**Confidence is the mechanism.** Lee et al., [The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c4e9dd153cff573990b70ac8a7640236/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (CHI 2025, 498 citations) surveyed 319 knowledge workers across 936 first-hand examples: **higher confidence in GenAI predicted less critical thinking; higher self-confidence predicted more.** They also document that GenAI shifts the *nature* of the work toward verification, integration, and task stewardship.

**The finding that should open your piece.** Tsakalerou et al., [AI-assisted design synthesis and human creativity](https://consensus.app/papers/details/85474fdb7a495fca8fe3ab7ebc1fd3cc/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Frontiers in AI, 2026). AI-assisted teams versus human-only teams on design challenges: AI **accelerated idea generation but encouraged premature convergence, narrowed exploration, and compromised functional refinement.** Human-only teams engaged in more iterative experimentation and produced higher functional quality and greater ideational diversity. And the kicker: **participants' self-perceptions of creativity remained stable across both conditions**, meaning the deficit was masked by unchanged confidence.

Pair that with Clinton's calibration finding and you have one argument twice: **the screen and the model both degrade the work while leaving your confidence intact.** Paper is a calibration device.

**And the case for structure specifically.** Gerlich, [From Offloading to Engagement](https://consensus.app/papers/details/43104c88c78e5f778db06ec3c5f5c7c2/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Data, 2025, n = 150 across three countries): **unguided AI use fostered cognitive offloading without improving reasoning quality, whereas structured prompting significantly reduced offloading and enhanced both critical reasoning and reflective engagement.** Vendrell et al. (2026) build a design framework on the same premise, with two principles that read like they were written for this project: **preserve cognitive friction**, and **sequence AI-mediated with AI-free phases.**

That last phrase is the academic name for the paper loop.

---

## The tension you should address head-on

Sio and Ormerod found that **high cognitive demand during the incubation period shrinks the incubation effect**. A dense worksheet is a cognitively demanding task. Taken naively, that finding argues against paper-session Deep mode.

The resolution is to stop calling the paper session an incubation period, because it isn't one. In Wallas's terms it is **preparation**, and Sio and Ormerod found that **longer preparation produces a larger subsequent incubation effect**. The worksheet loads the problem deliberately and deeply; the incubation happens afterward, in the shower, on the walk, overnight, before you scan.

This reframe also fixes your two-mode design:

- **Deep mode is preparation.** Effortful by design, and it earns its cost by making whatever follows more productive.
- **Light mode is closer to true low-demand incubation.** Couch, TV, one pen gesture per item. Which is exactly the condition Sio and Ormerod found *outperformed rest*.

Stated that way, the two modes aren't a UX convenience. They're two different stages of the Wallas model, and the skill happens to implement both.

---

## What the research does NOT support (say this out loud)

1. **No study tests this artifact.** There is no research on AI-generated structured worksheets completed offline and returned. Every claim here is assembled from adjacent literatures. Assembling them is a hypothesis, not a finding.
2. **Mind wandering as the mechanism is contested**, with a well-powered failed replication (N = 443).
3. **Handwriting's memory advantage is weaker than the popular telling.** The encoding and generalization evidence is good; simple recall superiority is not established.
4. **Screen inferiority is a default-mode effect**, absent when stakes already force depth. A tablet closes part of the gap.
5. **Most of this is undergraduates in labs.** The walking meta-analysis notes most participants were post-secondary students. Ecological validity is a standing limitation the researchers themselves flag.
6. **Nearly all of it is correlational on the AI side.** Gerlich's headline finding is a correlation; heavy AI users may differ in ways that predict both.

## Bonus cluster: the physical case (walking)

Directly relevant since your sessions happen on a patio, and this is the strongest effect size in the brief.

Thabane et al., [The impact of walking on creative thinking: A systematic review and meta-analysis](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1d1194de66635bfe807826c9ab9a8144/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (PLOS One, 2026), 23 studies, 1,036 participants: **moderate-certainty evidence of a large effect of walking on divergent thinking, d = 0.93**, holding at **d = 0.82 in randomized trials only**, and a null effect on convergent thinking (d = 0.16).

The origin study, Oppezzo and Schwartz's [Give your ideas some legs](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b2618fae3f8b5c20b9909f314d85ec56/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (JEP:LMC, 2014, 514 citations): **walking increased creativity for 81% of participants** on divergent thinking versus only 23% on convergent, the boost **persisted after sitting back down**, and **walking outdoors produced the most novel and highest-quality analogies**.

Two design implications: divergent-only (walking helps you generate, not decide), and the residual boost means a walk *before* a Deep sheet may be as valuable as the sheet itself.

---

## Three numbers for the talk

- **d = 0.93.** Walking's effect on divergent thinking, meta-analytic, 23 studies.
- **58%.** More ideas produced by employees given unexpected idle time, in a natural experiment at real work.
- **Confidence held steady.** In the AI-assisted design study, output quality and ideational diversity fell while participants' self-assessed creativity did not move. The same pattern appears in the paper-versus-screen calibration data. That is the thesis in one sentence: **the tools degrade the work and leave the confidence intact, and paper is where you find out.**

# Part Two: Session Mechanics

Added after the first brief. These bear on how to build a sheet rather than why to leave the screen.

## Cluster 6: Physical manipulation (the correction)

I previously called cut-apart cards "the highest-value thing paper offers." The evidence does not support that claim as stated, and the honest version is narrower.

**The phenomenon is real.** Kirsh and Maglio's [On Distinguishing Epistemic from Pragmatic Action](https://consensus.app/papers/details/790a51cb49075dfe975b6756e7f68d2a/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Cognitive Science, 1994, 1484 citations) established that people take physical actions whose purpose is to *uncover information that is hidden or hard to compute mentally*, distinct from actions that move toward a goal. Maglio and Kirsh later showed the cognitive benefit outweighs the time cost of the extra actions.

**But it does not generalize to problem solving.** Chuderski and colleagues ([British Journal of Psychology, 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e4b9e83c0c625c858d16ae48efdc5d92/?utm_source=claude_desktop)) gave 124 people physical interaction with nine insight problems against 124 working on paper and found **no general facilitating effect**; only one problem benefited, and use of hints was actively *hindered* by physical interaction. Macchi et al. ([2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ad5c4be464af5b57859bd5b4ada03dd7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)) found no advantage even for spatial insight problems, concluding the effect appears only where manipulation reveals a functional aspect crucial to restructuring. Schaefer (2025) found embodied manipulation reduced errors **only under cognitive load**, with no difference otherwise. Pouw et al. (2014) document a "moderate view" in which interactive richness can raise cognitive load and hinder transfer.

**The defensible claim:** physical arrangement earns its place when the task has many items, is relational or spatial, and exceeds working memory. That is exactly the course-arc case, and it is not most sheets. Cut-apart cards stay in the library with that scope. They are not a general creativity booster and should not be sold as one.

## Cluster 7: Sketching (the keystone)

This is the strongest paper-beats-screen mechanism in the entire brief, and unlike most of the others it comes with a direct comparative test.

**The comparative finding.** Stones and Cassidy, [Seeing and discovering](https://consensus.app/papers/details/93869bb379335da48e8ea4e4ca90c2f7/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Design Studies, 2010): students could reinterpret digital marks as other forms, but were **less successful at turning those new digital forms into new ideas**. Paper-based sketches, more than digital tools, supported the reinterpretation that generates new ideas. Not a claim about comfort or preference; a difference in idea production from the same cognitive move.

**Why it works: vagueness fights fixation.** Tversky and Suwa, [Thinking with Sketches](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0af0a0e948cb5747ad71e928f835fe3e/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (2009): sketching is integral to design because *vagueness encourages reinterpretation and discourages fixation*. This connects the sketching literature directly to the fixation literature. A loose drawing is a defixation device.

**The mechanism of discovery.** Suwa, Gero, and Purcell, [Unexpected discoveries and S-invention of design requirements](https://consensus.app/papers/details/41fe8c7dfa4557488a34f0042b83095e/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Design Studies, 2000, 451 citations): unexpected discoveries in sketches and the invention of new design requirements drive each other bidirectionally. Discoveries are most likely when the designer **attends simultaneously to elements that have never been attended to together**, and when a newly invented requirement supplies a fresh perspective.

**And it is trainable.** Suwa's work on *constructive perception* found that **novices instructed in the skill generated more interpretations from a single ambiguous drawing and showed a slower decline in generation over time**. This is directly actionable: a sketch prompt can instruct for reinterpretation rather than hoping for it.

**Boundary:** Tseng (2018) found higher ambiguity increased idea quantity for experienced designers but not novices. Verstijnen (1998) similarly finds expertise moderates.

## Cluster 8: Idea selection (now with actual answers)

Previously all I had was "selection performs at chance." The fixes are counterintuitive.

**Intuitive beats deliberative.** Zhu, Ritter, and colleagues, [Intuitive processing outperforms deliberative processing in creative idea selection](https://consensus.app/papers/details/31ed79f41cb35ffcb967aac5a9b560b4/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (JESP, 2017): participants told to select intuitively chose ideas that were more creative, more original, and equally useful as those chosen deliberatively. **Deliberative selection performed no better than chance; intuitive selection beat the average.** This argues directly against scoring matrices and weighted rubrics.

**Naming the criterion is the one instruction that works.** Rietzschel et al. ([British Journal of Psychology, 2010](https://consensus.app/papers/details/56ab32dc84dc524ebaec722e98f8c94a/?utm_source=claude_desktop), 381 citations): exclusion instructions and quality ratings had no effect, but **explicitly instructing people to select creative or original ideas did improve selection**, at the cost of their satisfaction with the choice. Reiter-Palmon et al. (2018) separately found rubrics did not increase accuracy and iterative selection produced *lower* quality.

**Rank-ordering mechanics don't matter.** Zhu et al. ([2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/507930f39239566682f3a7a534fcfbd4/?utm_source=claude_desktop)) compared choosing, elimination, and paired comparison across four experiments plus meta-analysis and found no difference, with Bayesian support for the null. Don't engineer the ranking.

**Psychological distance helps.** Zhang et al. ([BMC Psychology, 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/791be1280021553da900a59f008331f9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), four studies: greater psychological distance increased the originality of selected ideas and the rate of optimal selection, mediated by construal level. Same lever as the self-distancing finding in reflective writing, pointed at a different job.

**Never mention implementation.** Van Broekhoven et al. ([PLoS ONE, 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5c8ec74d31d257f9bfdcad67034f7f09/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), field experiment, N = 403: people told they would have to *build* the idea they selected turned away from original ideas toward feasible ones. Any mention of execution on a selection sheet corrupts the selection.

## Cluster 9: Analogical distance

**The core tradeoff.** Venkataraman et al. ([Journal of Mechanical Design, 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b6fef7a3962a5cc89d7ebb5982ede1db/?utm_source=claude_desktop)): as analogical distance increases, **novelty increases**; as distance decreases, **quality increases**. Match the distance to the sheet's job.

**Far plus uncommon is the strongest combination.** Chan et al. ([Journal of Mechanical Design, 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/992d3d6851ba52f28780b847a2b13e81/?utm_source=claude_desktop), 301 citations): far-field and less-common examples both produced positive effects on novelty, and the combination outperformed a no-example control. This is the design-fixation "single uncommon example" finding arriving from an independent literature.

**There is such a thing as too far.** Fu et al. ([2012](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9e6a0918d4855a7abafc14e8f226cf51/?utm_source=claude_desktop), 284 citations) found stimuli that are too distant become harmful. Chan et al. (2023) showed **bridging analogies**, which connect a familiar anchor to the far source, help people actually use distant inspiration.

**Explain shallowly.** Keshwani and Chakrabarti (2017): biological-domain analogies produced significantly greater novelty than cross- or in-domain analogies **for less comprehensive explanations**; with deep explanation the advantage disappeared. A one-line analogy beats a paragraph.

**Expertise moderates.** Jia et al. (2019) found designers untrained in design-by-analogy did better with *near* analogies. Far analogies are a tool for people who already know the domain.

**And the machine is good at this.** Ding et al. ([Creativity and Cognition, 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/386ae2d66bfc59d38035061fd5d36266/?utm_source=claude_desktop)): LLM-generated cross-domain analogies were rated helpful at a median of 4 out of 5 and produced observable changes in problem formulation in roughly 80% of cases. This is a rare case where AI contribution to a generative sheet is empirically supported rather than merely tolerated.

## Cluster 10: Desirable difficulties, and the adoption problem

**The framework holds; one popular application does not.** Font-based disfluency is largely debunked. Sans Forgetica failed to improve memory across four experiments ([Taylor et al., Memory, 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e7c2b01e5488566d96182a8f58ef7cb3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)) and again in a 2026 study of 165 participants that found no effect on achievement, retention, cognitive load, or judgments of learning. Where disfluency does anything, Seufert et al. (2017) found an inverted-U, and Beege et al. (2021) found it helps only when element interactivity is low and *hurts* when it is high. **Never make a Paper Session sheet harder to read on purpose.** Difficulty belongs in the task, never in the typography.

**The finding that matters most for adoption.** Kirk-Johnson et al., [Perceiving effort as poor learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/935d3d7c141f5bd4ad109cf882d2b62f/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (Cognitive Psychology, 2019, 125 citations): across three studies, participants who perceived a strategy as more effortful rated it as **less effective** and were **less likely to choose it**, while choosing the more effortful strategy was associated with **better long-term retention**. De Bruin et al. (2023) build a whole framework around this resistance.

This is the third appearance of the same pattern in this brief. On screen, comprehension drops and confidence holds. With AI assistance, idea quality and diversity drop and self-assessed creativity holds. And now: effortful methods work better and *feel* worse. The paper session will feel less productive than the screen even on the days it is most productive. That is a design problem for the artifact and a rhetorical problem for the thesis, and it is the same problem twice.

# Part Three: Field reports

**The tier below everything above.** Real sessions, reported by the people who ran them. This section exists so field data has a defined place to land — and a defined ceiling.

Rules for this tier:

- Every entry states its N and how it was collected. Most entries will be N = 1 and self-selected; say so.
- Field reports sit below the lab evidence, always. A report can flag a rule for review; it can never be the sole support for adding one. The bar in `CONTRIBUTING.md` still holds: a rule that changes what gets printed needs a source in the clusters above.
- Limitations up front, in the same register as "What the research does NOT support."
- What an entry records: date, capacity (Deep or Light), patterns used, what came back — completed, partial, or abandoned, and where it stopped — plus anything the scan-back pass misread.

**Threshold for action:** three independent reports agreeing on the same failure — the same pattern abandoned, the same zone misread, the same sheet type not coming back — justify opening a pattern-review issue. Until then, entries accumulate here.
